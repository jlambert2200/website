---
title: "On AI Safety and “Getting a Gap” Over China"
date: 2026-11-11
summary: "A US lead in AI is fine. Selling it as protection from a Chinese existential threat is the problem."
---

![Officials seated at a long table sign a treaty in a formal White House room](/blog/npt-signing-1968.jpg)

*The signing of the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty at the White House, July 1, 1968. Photo: LBJ Presidential Library.*

Some researchers in Washington work from a consensus that the US must build and hold a lead over China in AI. I think the lead itself is fine. My problem is with how it's being sold. Chinese AI is being treated as a risk larger than AI itself, when what's happening between the US and China is ordinary competition between two powers. Meanwhile, what is happening in frontier labs on our own soil is an unprecedented and unfolding risk scenario. The US lead over China won't last, and when it closes we will need China as a partner in gatekeeping dangerous applications of AI. For this, we can look to lessons learned from the history of nuclear weapons.

Chinese AI models are certainly not limited by talent or research capability. The IP theft streams and illicit tech transfers through covert Chinese operations are real and ongoing, but that shouldn't overshadow the novel innovation and invention Chinese universities and firms are capable of producing.

So how has a gap been created, and why are researcher-lobbyists on Capitol Hill so dead set on maintaining it? Hardware export controls, specifically over GPUs. China has power plants. It has developers. It doesn't have the golden ticket (compute). For politicians and the traditional national security elite, a lead on your competitor in a technological arms race is simply a good thing, no matter the context. For the new yet already influential cohort of AI safety experts, it is about US AI lab research reaching the point of existential risk before anyone else's does; presumably, this allows them to concoct the right policy solution before the apocalyptic technology goes global.

Let's return to the height of the nuclear arms race to recall the logic behind "being first," and whether it's wise. The United States and the Soviet Union acquired their weapons in the 1940s. The UK and France arrived in the following decade. At this point, the US began pressuring China not to develop nuclear capabilities, even considering military strikes on development facilities (see Eliezer Yudkowsky's comments on strikes against "rogue data centers"). The pressure tactic failed, and China joined the ranks of nuclear-armed nations.

Despite this, the Cuban Missile Crisis, and the introduction of the NPT in 1970, the US and the Soviet Union continued to develop nuclear arsenals that could wreak devastating havoc on the world. Interestingly, the US arsenal peaked in size in 1967, while the Soviet Union continued building its stockpile until 1986.

Are there lessons here? Perhaps not. After all, artificial intelligence isn't being developed exclusively by nation-states, and the technology isn't meant to be an intimidation tactic; rather, AI is and will continue to be deployed in every area of human life to speed progress and extend capabilities. This could include military strategy, cyber warfare, space domination, social engineering, bioweapon development, and the list goes on and on.

Even so, I would argue we can't fully dismiss the lessons from the height of the nuclear war era. For one, the idea of permanently maintaining a gap over your nearest competitor was a phantom. Even in the midst of red lines and rhetoric, a collective of nations came together to sign the Non-Proliferation Treaty. There were leaks. The A.Q. Khan network brought additional nuclear arsenals online. But there were also great wins. Experts warned that the risk of a nuclear terrorist attack after the fall of the Soviet Union (and the sudden jeopardizing of its dispersed nuclear arsenal) was 50 percent by 2015 (Graham Allison and Lugar Survey). There was no such attack.

We should acknowledge outright the dangerous precedent that the AI arms race is creating between the US and China. There are many irons in the fire (synthetic drug precursor chemicals, trade deficits and dumping, interest in Taiwan, support for Russia), all of which compound an already very complex relationship between the two countries. I'm not suggesting that Nvidia begin selling as many GPUs to the Chinese as it does in the US. I'm also not suggesting that the US should pretend it is not hoping to gain an edge over the rest of the world in developing AI. However, implying that Chinese development of AI is an existential risk and framing policy approaches as attempts to hamper China's progress is pouring salt in a wound unnecessarily. Rest assured, every level of AI prowess achieved in the US will arrive in China, probably much sooner than one would think. It is wasted energy to pretend that is not true.

As with the Soviet Union in the nuclear era, we can expect China to be a partner in the broader aim to preserve global peace and security. Even if we are less confident in its ability to develop safety measures than in our own, China will be a necessary partner in preventing AI from reaching dangerous non-state actors.

I'll add: some AI safety experts dislike the use of nuclear weapons as a model or analogy for AI governance strategy. However, even in my stance as someone in favor of accepting Chinese AI parity, I view dialogues on nuclear proliferation (such as the debates between Scott Sagan and Kenneth Waltz) as illustrative of how the nuclear model could just as well be used to argue in favor of universalizing AI tools among trusted nation-states.
