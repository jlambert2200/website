## Section 1: Introduction & Overview

Mexican authorities are struggling to deter cartels. Violence and criminal behavior have continued on an upward trajectory. In this essay, the latest attempts to deter these criminal organizations are evaluated by applying Ned Lebow's deterrence credibility formulation (Lebow, 1981). It finds that the Mexican government has succeeded and failed in various ways. Commitment and communication are weak and inconsistent, resulting in a deterrence failure as the framework correctly predicts. Capability and resolve, however, are demonstrably strong from the Mexican government, as evidenced by the February 2026 killing of CJNG leader “El Mencho” (Grant, 2026). Still, even with these important elements of deterrence in place, deterrence outcomes are not achieved. This gap between satisfied criteria and absent results forms the central puzzle of the essay. Mexico supplies a rare natural experiment. The state has delivered the very inputs that Lebow's model treats as sufficient for restraint, yet the predicted restraint never arrives. The case, therefore, pressures the framework at its strongest point.

The reason for this may be something Lebow and Stein identified themselves in 1987. Deterrence works only against opportunity-driven, gain-maximizing adversaries, and is “at best ineffective and at worst counterproductive” against actors driven by need, loss, and vulnerability (Lebow & Stein, 1987, p. 36). Cartels are the latter. When cartels face leadership decapitation or territorial loss, they respond to ensure their own survival and preserve their reputation rather than to maximize profits or seize growth opportunities. Decapitation puts a cartel in a loss frame. A threat to its existence raises its willingness to fight rather than lowering it.

The last dip in violence in Mexico came during the PRI era, a period of relative stability produced not by credible threat but by a reassurance regime of negotiated impunity (Trejo & Ley, 2020, p. 44–45). Lebow and Stein proposed such a regime as a possible substitute for coercive deterrence (Lebow & Stein, 1987, p. 6–7). The modern case of Mexico's deterrence dilemma tests Lebow's original findings, but it is more useful as a confirmation of the theoretical developments he and Stein made a few years later.

Recent events have demonstrated that Lebow's criteria can be satisfied, and deterrence still does not follow. The main criminal actors retaliate rather than restrain themselves when the Mexican state demonstrates capability and resolve. When Mexican authorities killed CJNG leader “El Mencho,” they encountered widespread aggression across a dozen states rather than retreat or submission (Buschschlüter, 2026). The 2024 removal of El Mayo Zambada led to the fragmentation of the Sinaloa Cartel into open war rather than deterring it (Arocha & Pellegrini, 2026). Lebow's framework assumes a unitary actor will respond to credible threats with restraint. In Mexico, the evidence runs in the opposite direction. The essay works through Lebow's four criteria, then turns to why two satisfied criteria still fail to deter, and finally to the reassurance regime sketched above, and weighs it against continued coercion.

## Section 2: The Four Criteria



### Commitment

The redlines set by the Mexican state have not been consistent. When President Andrés Manuel López Obrador adopted his policy of “abrazos no balazos,” he set an implicitly negative redline. It essentially promised to embrace the status quo, which included widespread criminal operations and territorial control, in exchange for restraining violence (Felbab-Brown, 2019). More recently, President Claudia Sheinbaum has taken a confrontational posture that led to the decapitation operation against El Mencho. Previous administrations were led by corrupt officials accepting money and turning a blind eye (Trejo & Ley, 2020, p. 42). Commitments exist, but their continuity has been short, and previous iterations have been either too vague or frequently violated with no response. Because commitment has been neither clearly defined nor sustained long enough to acquire credibility, deterrence failure is the result Lebow’s 1981 framework predicts. Lebow argues that a deterrer must carefully define the unacceptable behavior and that credibility is the “quintessential condition” for a deterrent threat to hold (Lebow & Stein, 1987, p. 8–9). A deterrer earns credibility across repeated encounters, and each unanswered violation spends down that reserve. Mexico has reversed the process. Successive administrations taught cartels that today's redline may dissolve into tomorrow's accommodation. The audience learns to wait out the threat rather than to obey it.

### Communication

Signals are sent and received, but the meanings are not shared. Decapitations, such as the killing of El Mencho, sent a clear message of resolve. The CJNG sent their reply with vehicle fires, blockades, and killings (Solis, 2026). These responses are their own kind of counter-deterrent signaling from the cartel communicating to the state that such actions will not be tolerated. In either direction, the violence fails to communicate the message, or is at least not interpreted as a demand for cessation of activities. Instead, it dares the other side to respond in kind, with each fearing the appearance of weakness. Janice Stein's solo work on deterrence against irrational adversaries explains that traditional deterrence frameworks, like Lebow's, presuppose that both sides share an interpretive frame (Stein, 2009). In the aftermath of El Mencho's death, both sides clearly did not share the same interpretation of what the state's actions meant for the CJNG. The state reads decapitation as a deterrent signal. The CJNG reads it as an existential threat requiring defensive escalation. That asymmetry of interpretive frames is the “no common knowledge” problem operating in practice. This breakdown resembles a security dilemma more than a bargaining failure. Each side reads the other's move as proof of hostile intent and answers in kind. The state escalates to restore deterrence. The cartel escalates to survive. Neither reading leaves room for the de-escalation that a shared frame would permit.

### Capability

The El Mencho operation proves that the Mexican state has adequate capability to follow through on commitments and inflict severe pain on its adversaries. The most-wanted man in the country was killed through a combination of special forces, intelligence, and advanced equipment (Argomedo, 2026). This capability is not new and was demonstrated in the capture of Ovidio Guzmán López in 2019 (Sieff, 2019). At Culiacán that same year, approximately 3,200 troops and air assets were available and not deployed (Sieff, 2019). The presence alone simply does not produce the restraint Lebow's framework anticipates. Capability favors the Mexican state, yet still does not offer a guarantee. The state can reach any target it chooses. Reaching that target settles nothing about whether the organization it strikes will then comply.

### Will and Resolve

If capability did not determine the outcome, will is the next place to look. Mexican resolve has ranged from collapse to firmness within a few years, yet even its firmest form failed to deter. The twist in 2019 is that despite effectively capturing the man known as “El Ratón,” the government released him within days to placate the unrest of Sinaloa cartel members across the country (Sieff, 2019). This may explain why the CJNG felt it could respond with impunity to the killing of El Mencho. In 2023, however, the government recaptured El Ratón and held firm (Nuño, 2023). The killing of El Mencho was a similar demonstration of resolve (it is not possible to walk back a decapitation in the same way you can walk back an arrest). The conclusion here is that maximum resolve produced escalation in violence rather than deterrence. Resolve of this kind carries a hidden cost. A decapitation narrows the bargaining range that an arrest preserves.

## Section 3: Why the Residual Failure Happens

Why does failure in some but not all parts of the Lebow framework produce a deterrence failure? From the four-criteria checklist, the capability and resolve criterion are met. Still, deterrence fails. This is the residual failure that the four-criteria checklist cannot explain. Traditional deterrence is partially applicable in the Mexico case, but it is also structurally inadequate for success against this type of non-state actor. Three structural failure modes explain the gap.

### Wrong adversary type

Deterrence assumes that adversaries are risk-prone gain-maximizers who exploit vulnerable commitments (what Lebow and Stein call the “theory of opportunity”) (Lebow & Stein, 1987, p. 6–7). Lebow and Stein show that dangerous challengers of state authority are usually driven instead by need: vulnerability, loss, and domestic pressure. Attempts to deter such actors can become “malignant by intensifying precisely those pressures that are pushing leaders toward a choice of force” (Lebow & Stein, 1987, p. 14). The wave of violence following El Mencho's killing is this dynamic exactly. It was a demonstration of a need for survival, reputation, and establishing legitimacy for the succeeding leadership. Put more directly, the decapitation operation intensified the violence it was meant to suppress.

Cartels operate in a frame of loss when leadership is attacked, and territory is seized. As the threat against those things rises, the willingness to fight increases rather than decreases. Lebow and Stein draw on the work of Kahneman and Tversky, who find that actors take larger risks to avoid loss than to seek gain (Lebow & Stein, 1987). A cartel facing the existential loss of leadership and territory is operating in precisely this mode. Prospect theory sharpens the prediction. Actors who sit below their reference point gamble to climb back toward it, and they accept odds that a gain-seeking rival would refuse. A cartel that has just lost its founder sits far below that point. Fighting the state becomes a reasonable gamble, and surrender becomes an unacceptable certain loss. The state's threat raises the stakes of the gamble without changing its direction.

### No off-ramp

Deterrence requires a tolerable status quo that the adversary can retreat to and survive. Mexico's security posture, reinforced by the US Foreign Terrorist Organization designation of CJNG and the Sinaloa Cartel in February 2025, explicitly frames the goal as elimination (Executive Order, 2025). There is no stated endpoint at which a cartel could comply and continue to exist in any form. In Schelling's terms, this converts deterrence into compellence. Deterrence prevents a future action, while compellence forces a change in ongoing existence (Schelling, 1966). Mexico is attempting the latter while calling it the former. Compellence against a non-state actor with no legal standing and no available negotiating channel is structurally different from, and harder than, deterrence in the interstate context Schelling and Lebow originally theorized. The asymmetry runs deeper still. A state that demands a rival's disappearance removes the rival's reason to cooperate. Compliance and resistance both end in elimination, so the cartel loses nothing by fighting. Mexico has accidentally equalized the payoff between submission and war, and a rational organization under those payoffs chooses war.

### No unitary adversary

The fragmentation produced by decapitation means there is no single actor to deter, no command structure to receive a signal, and no hierarchy to enforce restraint internally (Felbab-Brown, 2019; Stein, 2009). The El Mencho killing immediately raised the CJNG succession question: who receives the next deterrent signal? The El Mayo capture produced not a unified retaliatory response but internal Sinaloa fragmentation into Los Chapitos versus La Mayiza, with the two factions fighting each other rather than the state (Arocha & Pellegrini, 2026). These are two different fragmentation patterns from two decapitation events, which show the problem is structural rather than case-specific. Felbab-Brown found that high-value targeting has fragmented the criminal market so badly that even cartels themselves “lack clarity about the balance of power among them” (Felbab-Brown, 2019, p. 2). That is a conflict theater in which no deterrent signal can land cleanly. Decapitation also corrupts the feedback loop that deterrence depends on. A threat works only when the target survives to learn from it and to adjust. Killing the leader removes the one actor who could absorb the lesson and impose it on subordinates. The successors inherit the territory and the grievance without inheriting the memory of the deal that restrained their predecessor.

A critic could answer that these failures reflect poor execution and not a flaw in the theory. On this view, Mexico simply applied deterrence badly, and a more consistent state could still make traditional threat-only deterrence successful. This is perhaps true in Mexico for commitment and communication, where better statecraft would help. However, no amount of consistency converts a fragmented adversary into a unitary one, and no clarity of signaling supplies an off-ramp that the state's own goals have removed. Therefore, the structure of the Mexican government’s struggle against the cartels, not the decision-making alone, makes the four-criteria model an insufficient approach.

## Section 4: Punishment, Denial, and Reassurance

Recognizing that the Mexican government's approach only satisfies two of Lebow's four criteria, and that the structural conditions identified above would undermine deterrence even if all four were met, we turn to whether denial or reassurance approaches offer a more adequate framework than threat measures alone.

Mexico's denial efforts include National Guard territorial deployment, financial interdiction through asset seizure, and border interdiction with US cooperation (Marroquin Bitar, 2025). Expert assessments found that they are weak. Fragmented criminal markets re-route around enforcement nodes. Disrupting one group displaces the crime rather than eliminating it (Felbab-Brown, 2019, p. 1–2). Snyder's foundational formulation of deterrence by denial assumes you can physically block access to an objective (Snyder, 1961). That logic is harder to sustain against a distributed profit network than against a territorial military objective. Denial assumes a chokepoint. A profit network routes around chokepoints by design. Every seized shipment teaches the organization where the next one should travel, so enforcement trains new adaptations rather than stopping the problem.

If neither punishment nor denial can hold the line, reassurance becomes the remaining option. Lebow and Stein's Beyond Deterrence proposes reassurance as the alternative to deterrence, reducing the adversary's need rather than raising its costs, through reciprocity, self-restraint, and tacit norms of competition (Lebow & Stein, 1987, p. 5–6). Two cases in Mexico's recent history map onto this framework.

AMLO's “abrazos no balazos” doctrine and the accompanying Bienestar social investment programmes attempted to reduce cartel recruitment by attacking economic need in marginalized communities (Felbab-Brown, 2019). The honest evaluation is that it was poorly executed, and reassurance of this kind presupposes an adversary primarily concerned with security rather than one driven by profit at the organizational level (McCormick & Sandin, 2019).

The stronger historical example is the PRI era. Emerging scholarship on organized criminal groups agrees that while collusive or permissive state responsiveness to organized crime lacks traditionally recognized elements of deterrence, the results can sometimes produce more limited conflict levels (Trejo & Ley, 2020, pp. 44–45). Trejo and Ley argue that democratization broke this equilibrium by introducing electoral competition, which raised uncertainty about state protection and triggered criminal wars (Trejo & Ley, 2020, p. 49–58). Violence was suppressed not by threat but by mutual restraint enforced through shared interest in stability. The cartel-side analogue is El Mayo Zambada, whose restraint strategy kept Sinaloa violence relatively contained for years (Arocha & Pellegrini, 2026). His removal ended that internal equilibrium in the same way democratization ended the PRI-era one.

The state cannot rebuild collusion. Democratic consolidation, the US FTO designations, and US-assisted operations have foreclosed it politically and legally. Punishment backfires for the structural reasons identified in Section 3. Reassurance is difficult to run against a profit-seeking organization that does not primarily need state protection. This is the unresolved deterrence dilemma Mexico faces, and it is one that the existing theoretical toolkit handles poorly.

## Section 5: Conclusion

Mexico's cartel conflict does not refute Lebow's framework for credible deterrence, though the failures challenge it. Lebow and Stein's later work on what makes a deterrable adversary, however, is well-demonstrated by the conflict. Where commitment was weak, the 1981 framework correctly predicted failure. Where capability and resolve were demonstrably met, the framework predicted restraint and produced escalation instead.

Three structural factors provide a more robust explanation for failure that the checklist cannot supply: cartels are need-driven actors in a loss frame, for whom threats intensify rather than deter; Mexico's unlimited goals designate the status quo as intolerable, converting deterrence into compellence; and decapitation strategies fragment rather than discipline the adversary, dissolving the unitary actor Lebow's original framework requires. These are precisely the conditions Lebow and Stein identified in 1987 as the boundaries of deterrence theory's applicability.

The PRI era shows that the current question for non-state deterrence is not how to make threats more credible but whether a reassurance or norms regime can be reconstructed without collusion. A theory of deterrence adequate to this case must go beyond Lebow's four criteria to account for non-unitary adversaries, loss-frame dynamics, the absence of an off-ramp, and the structural factors that determine whether any deterrent posture can work at all. Future frameworks fit for non-state violence must therefore treat the adversary's frame, structure, and exit options as prior questions, and it must answer them before the four criteria become relevant at all.
