*Adapted from my dissertation, written in mid-2026.*

## Strikes and ambushes, 2012–2017

The violent and territorial nature of the CJNG invited heavy enforcement responses from the Mexican state. Erick Valencia Salazar (an alleged co-founder of the Jalisco cartel) was arrested in 2012, not long after the group first announced itself to the public (Partida et al., 2012). Rubén Oseguera and Antonio Oseguera Cervantes (El Mencho’s brother) were arrested separately in 2015, although Antonio was released the following year on a technicality (Janowitz, 2015; Office of Public Affairs US Department of Justice, 2025). Los Cuinis' leadership was similarly targeted, with brothers Jose, Gerardo, and Abigael Gonzalez-Valencia all arrested between 2015 and 2017. (Valle-Lopez, 2018)

By Spring of 2015, the CJNG and state forces were embroiled in a constant battle of ambush and retaliation, leading to the death of regional Jalisco Cartel leader “El Gringo” and an ambush that killed fifteen police outside of Puerto Vallarta (Martinez, 2015). In May of that year, Rubén Oseguera and his father, El Mencho, were being pursued by Mexican law enforcement via helicopter. Cartel members fired on the helicopter, bringing it down with a rocket-propelled grenade launcher and machine gun rounds (Office of Public Affairs US Department of Justice, 2025; Yang, 2022). This was an unprecedented event marking the first time a helicopter had been shot out of the sky by a criminal group in Mexico (Arévalo, 2026).

## The retreat, 2018–2024

The following years would bring something completely unexpected. Following the years of extreme state-cartel violence, and despite the successful capture of the head of the Sinaloa Cartel (Joaquín Guzmán Loera or “El Chapo”) in 2016, the political mandate to pursue DTOs by any means began to weaken. Andrés Manuel López Obrador, or AMLO, was elected President of Mexico in 2018, bringing with him the now-infamous “abrazos no balazos” non-confrontational stance. This included an overhaul of federal policing, an emphasis on justice reforms, anti-poverty measures, and reduced kingpin targeting (McCormick & Sandin, 2019). The consequence of AMLO’s unique approach was best encapsulated by his government’s shocking retreat after capturing the son of El Chapo, Ovidio Guzmán López. Sinaloa cartel members unleashed a wave of violence, causing authorities to balk at the threats and choose to release Ovidio Guzmán López. When the media questioned the strategy, AMLO simply replied, “You can’t fight fire with fire” (McGinnis, 2019). Against this backdrop, the Jalisco cartel continued to operate, playing a significant role in the record-breaking homicide rates achieved during the early AMLO years (World Bank Open Data, 2026). Despite some predicting (including Nathan Jones) that this would be a period of stepping back for the CJNG to refocus on corruption efforts over violence, there were still massacres of state police directly attributed to Jalisco cartel squads, as the government did less to engage the fighters (Jones, 2018, p. 24; BBC, 2020a; Reuters, 2019).

The CJNG’s war against state enforcement reached a fever pitch in 2020 with three major assassination attempts. In June of that year, hitmen from the group attempted to assassinate Mexico City’s chief of police, Omar García Harfuch, and killed Mexican Judge Uriel Villegas Ortiz and his wife (Dalby & Dittmar, 2020). (Villegas was the judge who, after multiple releases approved by five other judges, ordered the imprisonment of El Mencho’s son) (Fuentes, 2020). A few months later, the former governor of Jalisco, Aristóteles Sandoval, was murdered by suspected CJNG assassins (Bravo, 2020).

The state response to these escalations was mixed. The Harfuch assassination attempt was the most heavily prosecuted; however, efforts were led by the local government rather than the federal government (BBC, 2020b). However, as of 2025, the killing of Sandoval has gone virtually unresolved, with zero perpetrators directly involved arrested or sentenced for the attack (Loza, 2025). One arrest over the Villegas Ortiz killing was announced in 2020, but the public record lacks any evidence that charges or sentences were handed out (El Universal, 2020). In the words of Brookings expert Vanda Felbab-Brown, AMLO “essentially gave up on tackling [the cartels]” (Felbab-Brown, 2021).

## The return, 2024–2026

The inauguration of Claudia Sheinbaum in 2024 brought changes to the avoidant enforcement posture at the national level. Omar García Harfuch became part of Sheinbaum’s cabinet and led a return to heavy law enforcement and responses to violence (Press, 2024). The domestic political refresh would coincide with increased intensity and attention from the United States, which, under President Trump, designated major Mexican cartels as foreign terrorist organizations (FTOs) and pledged to do more in intelligence sharing (The White House, 2025; United States Department of State, 2025).

With arrests up and murder rates down at the start of 2026, it was difficult to judge how much the CJNG’s organizational capacity had waned (if at all). (El Universal, 2026; Reuters, 2026). February 2026 brought some answers when Mexican state security forces stormed a ranch in Tapalpa believed to be the hideout of El Mencho. A firefight broke out, and El Mencho died in state custody from wounds sustained during the shooting (Al Jazeera, 2026). The CJNG's reaction was severe. Hundreds of narcoblockades across twenty states went up immediately. Major cities such as Guadalajara came to a standstill as businesses and vehicles were set ablaze (Pellegrini, 2026). Twenty-five members of the Mexican National Guard were killed in retaliation, not counting those who died in the efforts to capture El Mencho before his death. Though the death of El Mencho might have been considered a boon for the Mexican state’s efforts to dismantle the CJNG, that win came with a high cost. The immense display of force put to rest questions about how widely the CJNG’s presence and firepower were distributed across the country.
