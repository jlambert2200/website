*Adapted from my dissertation, written in mid-2026.*

In 2016, Nathan Jones predicted that two major illegal drug trafficking organizations (also known as DTOs or cartels) in Mexico would soon fragment and dissolve (Jones, 2016, pp. 101, 114). These groups, Los Zetas and the Cártel Jalisco Nueva Generación (CJNG), both fit a typology he called “territorial,” which Jones identified as susceptible to fragmentation and dissolution (Jones, p. 35). Ten years later, one of his predictions held strong as Los Zetas experienced fragmentation, losing strength and resilience. The CJNG, however, has defied these expectations completely, rising to become arguably the most powerful DTO in Mexico, if not the world (Henkin, 2020, p. 1). This leads to the central question: Why did the CJNG survive and expand despite adopting the confrontational, territorial strategy that fragmented Los Zetas?

The answer is that the CJNG's success does not entirely falsify Jones’s theory; rather, it identifies the specific conditions under which his theory of resilience is suspended, allowing for exceptions. Up until 2018, the CJNG endured kingpin strikes under the same government that collapsed Los Zetas. While organizational innovation and secured resources enabled their survival during those years, a collapse in state willingness for six years halted the expected chain reaction toward collapse, allowing the cartel to shift from survival to expansion.

## What Jones predicted

Jones claims that territorial cartels are more visible and violent, provoking the state to crack down with measures like kingpin strikes (Jones, 2016, p. 35). State repression against a violent cartel results in fragmentation or total dissolution. The causal mechanism at play in Jones’ theory is more complex than a simple cartel-behavior-then-state-repression sequence. His explanation begins when territorial cartels target ordinary people; for example, they kidnap small business owners or doctors for ransom rather than other people in the drug trade (Jones, p. 87). This produces a public backlash. Communities and citizen groups may come together to demand a stronger punitive response than would otherwise occur. As political pressure mounts, politicians are forced to respond against criminal groups (Jones, p. 77). Law enforcement cracks down with a heavy hand, and the cartel is weakened, fragmented, or destroyed. This chain of events results from Mexico's democratic transition and the spread of civil society shaping political will (Jones, p. 20)

Jones relies on at least three important presuppositions to justify his causal logic. First, territorial cartels have hierarchical structures vulnerable to kingpin strikes; with visible leadership, removing the leader would cause fracturing, infighting, or even dissolution. Next, the theory assumes the state’s enforcement capability exceeds any cartel’s ability to resist, and the state will win any prolonged violent struggle. Finally, the state’s willingness to confront cartels rises with democratization and does not fall back. Jones traces this evolution through Mexican Presidents Salinas, Zedillo, Fox, and Calderón (Jones, p. 78).

## Why the first strikes failed

The Cuinis are a distinct family network handling finance and laundering, so strikes on the armed hierarchy did not reach the money, and strikes on the Cuinis did not reach the armed structure. In contrast, when authorities unmasked Los Zetas' money-laundering schemes, they found the Treviño brothers personally running both command and finance. The CJNG’s unique control over its expansion groups’ alliances maintains a strategic coherence that allows greater insulation from the chaotic world of intercartel warfare. In comparison, claims that Los Zetas were a franchising organization were overstated.

## Why the cartel then expanded

Under Calderón and Peña Nieto, both cartels received leadership strikes within the core family or group. Between 2018 and 2020, the CJNG's violence against the state rose, with the attempt on Harfuch, the killings of Villegas and Sandoval, and massacres of state police, while federal repression fell with the release of Ovidio Guzmán, the unresolved Sandoval and Villegas cases, and the Harfuch prosecution left to local authorities. When federal willingness returned under Sheinbaum, El Mencho was killed within fourteen months. Structure and finance explain why the CJNG survived the first round of strikes, while six years without repression explains why they were able to expand.

## What this means for the theory

The theory’s predictions hold when its three presuppositions are met; the problem is that Jones treated them as inevitable rather than conditional. The true mechanism leads to fragmentation of territorial cartels only when federal willingness to apply consistent pressure is sustained long enough. Where willingness lapses, the mechanism for breaking down territorial cartel resilience is suspended.

Nathan Jones' cartel resilience theory identified the right causal mechanism. Where he and those who shared his logic went wrong was in predicting that a territorial cartel could not survive sustained violent confrontation with the state. The CJNG case forces a reevaluation of that prediction and of the assumptions beneath it. The comparison confirms that Jones' logic holds, but that neither cartel structure nor state willingness is as fixed as he assumed. An innovative organizational structure and a six-year retreat from federal enforcement together reversed the trend toward fragmentation or dissolution that his theory expected for the Jalisco cartel.

Theories of cartel resilience must therefore account for two things. First, DTO structures and capacities are dynamic and will continue to change. Second, democratic politicians can walk back state enforcement as easily as they can escalate it. For the study of cartels, this means more case-specific research and less reliance on broad generalizations drawn from examples that are quickly becoming outdated. Predicting the future of Mexican cartels remains as difficult as it has ever been.
