*Adapted from my dissertation, written in mid-2026.*

The CJNG's organizational structure follows a clear hierarchy at the top while maintaining a flexible, innovative franchise model at lower levels across territories under its control (Office of the Director of National Intelligence, 2026). El Mencho and Los Cuinis (the Valencia family network) were identified as the leadership structure of the cartel and sanctioned by the United States DOJ in 2015. Los Cuinis’ connection to El Mencho was solidified when he married Rosalinda Gonzalez-Valencia, and following his death in 2026, Rosalinda’s son has filled the power vacuum as the declared successor to the chief role in the CJNG (Office of Public Affairs US Department of Justice, 2021; U.S. Department of the Treasury, 2026b). Los Cuinis have developed into a sophisticated criminal group focused on financial crime, financing the CJNG’s expansion efforts with legitimate business operations, including music, restaurant, and retail ventures, able to launder illicit revenue (Office of Public Affairs US Department of the Treasury, 2018; US Department of the Treasury, 2020)

## Three innovations on the franchise model

The franchise model for local cartel operations existed before the CJNG rose to prominence. However, the CJNG has developed innovative strategies to increase coherence across decentralized local operations and its central command (Mencho and Los Cuinis). First, the CJNG has “adopted” smaller factions of formerly stronger cartels, such as the Tijuana and Juárez cartels, among others (InSight Crime, 2026). This allows them to “expand” without moving much beyond branding and communication structures. Second, the Jalisco cartel avoids building a complicated web of sub-alliances (alliances between a franchisee and another party), instead directing alliances back to the core hierarchy (Jones et al., 2022, p. 14). By keeping newly formed branches operating within a silo, the risk of defection lowers even as day-to-day operations are self-directed. The most significant CJNG alliance has been a paradigm-shifting cooperation with the Chapitos faction of the Sinaloa Cartel (former enemies of the CJNG) in their internal war against supporters of Ismael “El Mayo” Zambada (N+, 2026). The third innovation the Jalisco Cartel has adopted is new technological systems that streamline and advance operations, reducing reliance on heavy manpower (typically required for territorial-type business strategies). Evidence includes the use of weaponized unmanned aircraft systems, or drones, for attacks on security personnel and surveillance, replacing human lookouts and the high-risk use of sicarios (Doctor et al., 2025, p. 4). Images have surfaced of military-style patches identifying “droneros” who are part of the CJNG’s technically skilled membership (Bunker & Sullivan, 2023). All of these allow semi-autonomous franchises to conduct their business without risking human capital or needing to grow headcount.

## Recruitment and the brand

In speaking about the CJNG’s use of technology, it is critical to address their sophisticated use of social media as both an appendage to the cartel’s strategy of terror as well as a tool for recruitment. Odio y Concordia Lab, along with the Civic AI Lab at Northeastern University, developed a research methodology to identify how social media, particularly TikTok, was used by the CJNG to target young people, students, and single mothers for recruitment (New Frontiers in Digital Recruitment, 2025, p. 2). Of the accounts identified as cartel-linked, the CJNG made up for fifty-four percent of the total. Posts from these accounts focused heavily on recruitment and general propaganda (New Frontiers in Digital Recruitment, 2025, pp. 15–16). Secretary Harfuch's comments about the detention of CJNG recruitment chief José Gregorio “N” described a process of forced recruitment in which respondents to falsely advertised job posts on social media were directed onto buses and sent to training camps near Rancho Izaguirre and the city of Teuchitlán (López, 2025). Meanwhile, the non-recruiting content promoted the cartel's glamorous and fearsome image to millions. By shifting cult-following away from the kingpin and emphasizing the brand, the Jalisco cartel has built a persona that may be hard to dissolve, even with kingpin arrests and heavy law enforcement.

## Synthetic drugs and the money

What about the Jalisco cartel’s position in the drug trade? Synthetic drug seizures by the DEA have exploded, with an over five-hundred percent increase in fentanyl seizures since 2019. DEA Administrator Derek Maltz said that El Mencho’s son, Ruben Oseguera-Gonzalez, was “one of the earliest architects of fentanyl trafficking...” (Office of Public Affairs US Department of Justice, 2025a). In 2025, the US Treasury sanctioned the CJNG, alleging that fentanyl precursor chemicals were flowing through the port of Manzanillo (which is believed to be under the cartel’s control) (US Department of the Treasury, 2025). The DEA reports the CJNG brings in billions of dollars via the manufacturing of synthetic drugs and cocaine trafficking (US Drug Enforcement Agency, 2026). The takeoff in demand for and ease of production for synthetic drugs has been coupled with increasing ease of moving money across borders. The CJNG has been documented working through brokers directing funds through cryptocurrencies, a complex process often involving affiliates in China in addition to the US and Latin America (Office of Public Affairs US Department of Justice, 2025b; TRM, 2025).
