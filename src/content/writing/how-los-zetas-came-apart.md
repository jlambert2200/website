*Adapted from my dissertation, written in mid-2026.*

## A cartel built on territory

A few significant pieces of evidence highlight that Los Zetas qualified as a territorial cartel. First, there is their military pedigree, which predisposed them to utilizing violence as a means of driving business. Los Zetas originated in the late nineties, when personnel from the Grupo Aeromóvil de Fuerzas Especiales (or GAFE, an elite special forces branch of the Mexican military) deserted their military assignments and were subsequently recruited by Osiel Cárdenas Guillén as enforcers for the Gulf Cartel (Insight Crime, 2024). Even before breaking away to become their own DTO, Los Zetas were notorious for extortion and kidnapping (core territorial business strategies). Court testimony from former Zetas confirmed that sicarios (or Zeta “soldiers”) existed to kidnap, execute, and extort. This established a fearsome reputation, allowing the Zetas to control plazas ("Control...Over the Entire State of Coahuila”, 2017, p. 14; Mosso, 2010). Unclassified FBI intelligence reports that in 2005, the group had established control over Nuevo Leon, exercising power over the city’s police force, while still operating as an appendage to the Gulf Cartel (Los Zetas: An Emerging Threat to the United States, 2005, p. 16). When the group broke from the Gulf Cartel in 2010, open war spread across Tamaulipas and Nuevo Léon (Mosso, 2010). This was because Los Zetas sought territorial control to establish their own revenue streams.

Additional evidence further confirms Los Zetas' territorial approach. Migrant kidnapping and extortion were reported multiple times, most significantly in 2010 when seventy-two migrants were found to have been executed in the town of San Fernando after the sole survivor managed to find a Mexican Navy checkpoint. US State Department archival sources claim this extreme violence suggests that publicity for Zeta's actions must have been useful to them (Unclassified US State Department Memo, 2011, p. 3). Indeed, Los Zetas showed little interest in using the migrants as drug mules, instead taking the moment as an opportunity to demonstrate a level of shocking violence to solidify their terrifying reputation, thereby earning themselves a greater ability to exercise territorial control in the region. With territorial control established, Los Zetas extended their revenue extraction beyond human targets to include natural resources, seizing fuel from Pemex infrastructure and taking control of coal extraction in the state of Coahuila (Vice News, as cited in Cawley, 2014; René, 2012).

## Violence without fear of the law

They had an extraordinary capacity for violence, demonstrating little fear of the law and confidence in their own ability to act with impunity. Besides the aforementioned San Fernando Massacre, Los Zetas have been allegedly responsible for the 2011 arson at the Casino Royale in Monterrey where fifty-two died, the dumping of forty-nine bodies on a highway outside Cadereyta, and the Allende massacre, where over one hundred (potentially up to three hundred) men, women, and children were brutally slaughtered (BBC News, 2011; Press, 2012; U.S. Attorney’s Office, Eastern District of Texas, 2021). In a wild display of insurgency, Zeta prisoners in a Monterrey prison killed forty-four Gulf cartel inmates. Afterward, thirty-five Zetas escaped from the jail (allegedly helped by corrupt guards) (Grillo, 2012).

## The state's campaign

The Mexican government, with support from the United States, took strong action against Los Zetas. Following the “kingpin” approach of targeting cartel leaders, SEMAR and SEDENA (the Mexican Secretariat of the Navy and of National Defense, respectively) worked to target Los Zetas leaders identified as critical actors in the network. In 2010, SEMAR conducted an operation leading to the arrest of José Ángel Fernández de Lara Díaz (alias “El Pelón), the Zeta boss in Quintana Roo, along with his assistant in charge of administration and finances (Secretaría de la Defensa Nacional, 2010). One of SEDENA's most comprehensive operations, “Operación Escorpión,” led to 724 arrests, including Carlos Oliva Castillo (an important leader just below Lazcano and the Treviños) (Secretaría de la Defensa Nacional, 2011a). This successful raid was notable for its explicitly stated aims of weakening the cartel's command, financial, operational, and logistical structures. While the kingpin strategy remained largely unchanged from its early implementation in Colombia in the 1990s, the Mexican federal government deliberately aimed to weaken the entire cartel, not simply cause chaos through disputed succession. As time progressed, additional key leadership strikes were executed as SEMAR killed Lazcano on the 7th of October 2012, and the Treviño brothers were arrested on the 15th of July 2013 (Miguel Ángel) and on the 4th of March 2015 (Omar) (Insight Crime, 2024).

## The collapse

Despite their huge geographic presence, sophisticated command and intelligence systems, and penetration of public institutions, the extremely violent Los Zetas cartel could not recover from the repeated kingpin strikes, eventually succumbing to the state pressure and fragmenting into weaker factions. The most notable successor factions, Zetas Vieja Escuela and Cártel del Noreste, are only shadows of the original DTO, which commanded the public attention and dominated their rivals (Asmann, 2018). Correa-Cabrera described the splintering as a gradual process, emphasizing the killing of Lazcano and the arrests of the Treviño brothers as well as the militarization of Mexico’s war on organized crime (Correa-Cabrera, as cited in Asmann, 2018). Security analyst Alejandro Hope, for El Universal, agreed that “the Zetas as a coherent national organization ceased to exist after the arrest of Z-40 [Miguel Angel Treviño Morales]” (Hope, 2018).

In effect, the results aligned well with Nathan Jones’ theory of cartel resilience. The Zetas' business model, which emphasized violent and extractive territorial actions, drew extra attention from the Mexican and United States governments. After a couple of shaky successions, the group finally cracked; not in a single moment, but over a couple of years, the original cartel was no longer the same.
